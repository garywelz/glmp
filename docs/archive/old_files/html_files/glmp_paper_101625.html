The 100:12:6:2 Pattern: Evidence for a Conserved Computational Architecture in Prokaryotic and Eukaryotic Core Processes Systematic Analysis of 6,467 Computational Nodes Across 100 Well-Characterized Cellular Processes Provides Evidence for Evolution's Optimization of Efficiency, Robustness, Integration, and Control Gary Welz Department of Mathematics and Computer Science John Jay College of Criminal Justice City University of New York (CUNY) 524 West 59th Street New York, NY 10019 (Retired) Email: gwelz@jjay.cuny.edu GitHub: https://github.com/garywelz Hugging Face: https://huggingface.co/garywelz Abstract Comprehensive analysis of 6,467 computational nodes across 100 well-characterized cellular processes in bacteria and yeast reveals evidence for a conserved computational pattern: for every 100 IF-THEN conditional steps (sequential biochemical progression), 12 OR gates, 6 AND gates, and 2 NOT gates occur — a 100:12:6:2 architecture suggesting evolution's optimization for flexibility over specificity. The Genome Logic Modeling Project (GLMP) introduces a novel approach to biological process visualization through the application of computational programming frameworks. By representing biological systems as executable programs with standardized color-coded components, this methodology enables systematic analysis of complex biological processes across bacteria and eukaryotes. We present a curated dataset of 100 processes (64 E. coli, 33 S. cerevisiae, 3 B. subtilis) with complete citation to peer-reviewed literature. This architecture reflects evolution's optimization for efficiency (linear pathways dominate at 84.6%), robustness (OR gates at 9.9% provide redundancy), integration (AND gates at 5.4% enable signal convergence), and control (NOT gates at 1.9% prevent wasteful processes). Genomic budget calculations suggest that if non-coding DNA serves as the substrate for logical regulation, these computational elements could account for approximately 60% of prokaryotic and 12% of eukaryotic non-coding regions — a testable prediction that provides a functional hypothesis for non-coding DNA. All processes are stored in a PostgreSQL research database and publicly accessible through https://storage.googleapis.com/regal-scholar-453620-r7-podcast-storage/glmp-database-table.html. Keywords: computational biology, process visualization, synthetic biology, biological programming, systems biology, experimental validation Introduction The representation of biological processes has evolved significantly with the advent of computational tools and visualization technologies5,6. Traditional biological diagrams often lack the systematic structure needed for computational analysis, while established standards like the Systems Biology Graphical Notation (SBGN) require significant expertise and time to create properly7,8,21. This creates a barrier for researchers who need rapid visualization of biological processes for hypothesis generation, experimental design, and cross-disciplinary collaboration. The Genome Logic Modeling Project (GLMP) addresses these workflow gaps by introducing a standardized "Programming Framework" methodology that represents biological processes as computational programs with clearly defined inputs, processing steps, and outputs1,2. This approach leverages the universal nature of computational logic to identify patterns across diverse biological systems, from viral replication mechanisms to neural decision-making processes9,10. By applying consistent color-coding and structural conventions, the framework enables researchers to recognize computational similarities between seemingly unrelated biological processes, opening new avenues for synthetic biology design and experimental validation11,12. GLMP serves as a rapid prototyping tool that bridges the gap between natural language descriptions of biological processes and formal visualization standards. It democratizes pathway visualization by making it accessible to researchers without specialized training in complex diagramming tools, while maintaining compatibility with established standards like SBGN. The conceptual foundation for viewing genomes as computational programs was first proposed in 19952-4, and this work builds upon those early insights with modern visualization and analysis tools to address real workflow needs in the biological research community. Methods Programming Framework Methodology The Programming Framework employs a five-category color-coding system to represent biological processes as computational programs1: Red: Triggers & Inputs Yellow: Catalysts & Enzymes Green: Chemical Processing Blue: Intermediates & States Orange: OR Logic Gates Lavender: AND Logic Gates Violet: Products & Outputs Visualization Generation Flowcharts were generated using Mermaid diagram syntax created by large language models (LLMs) in response to text descriptions of biological processes17,20,18. Each process is stored as a structured JSON file containing metadata (organism, category, citations), complexity metrics (node counts, logic gate counts), and the Mermaid diagram code as a text string. The interactive viewer (JavaScript-based) fetches these JSON files from Google Cloud Storage (GCS), extracts the Mermaid code, and renders flowcharts in-browser via the Mermaid.js library (v10.6.1), enabling version-controlled, text-native, and scalable visualizations without requiring specialized diagram software. Process Selection Criteria To address potential selection bias, processes were chosen using systematic criteria independent of logic gate characteristics: Textbook coverage: Processes documented in major biochemistry and molecular biology textbooks (Alberts et al., Molecular Biology of the Cell; Lehninger Principles of Biochemistry) Literature support: Minimum of 2 peer-reviewed primary research articles with experimental validation Mechanistic completeness: Well-understood pathways with documented molecular mechanisms (not fragmentary or speculative) Category diversity: Representation across major functional categories (metabolism, gene regulation, DNA/RNA processes, stress responses, signal transduction) Model organism focus: Core processes in well-studied organisms (E. coli, S. cerevisiae, B. subtilis) Critical note on bias prevention: Processes were NOT selected based on logic gate ratios or computational architecture, as the 100:12:6:2 pattern was unknown during selection and emerged only through subsequent analysis. The first 30 processes were analyzed without preconception of any pattern; the observed architecture was then validated in the remaining 67 processes. Selection aimed to represent canonical biological knowledge, not to confirm a hypothesis. Validation Against Biological Literature To ensure logic gates represent biological reality rather than classification artifacts, all gate assignments were validated against primary literature and established biological mechanisms: OR gates validated against documented alternatives: Alternative DNA repair pathways (RecFOR vs RecBCD in E. coli, extensively characterized in Kuzminov 1999; Clark & Sandler 1994), multiple sigma factor promoters (σ70 vs σ32 vs σE activation routes documented in Gross et al. 1998), and parallel metabolic routes validated against biochemistry textbooks and primary research. AND gates validated against multi-component requirements: The lac operon AND gate (strong transcription requires LacI release AND CAP-cAMP binding) was originally described by Jacob & Monod (1961) and confirmed by decades of experimental work. Ribosome assembly requiring multiple r-proteins AND rRNA processing (Kaczanowska & Rydén-Aulin 2007), chaperone systems requiring DnaK+DnaJ+GrpE (Mayer & Bukau 2005), and signal transduction cascades requiring multi-component assembly are all experimentally validated. NOT gates validated against characterized repressors: Repressor systems (LacI in Swint-Kruse & Matthews 2009; TrpR in Lawson et al. 2004; AraC in Schleif 2010; Gal80 in Lohr et al. 1995) are classical genetics examples with crystal structures and detailed biochemical characterization. Pattern emergence documentation: The 100:12:6:2 architecture was not hypothesized a priori. Initial analysis of 30 diverse processes (selected to span different categories and organisms) revealed the pattern, which was then consistently observed in the subsequent 67 processes. This emergence from unbiased initial analysis, rather than confirmation of a pre-existing hypothesis, supports the pattern's biological authenticity. All logic gate assignments correspond to experimentally validated biological mechanisms documented in peer-reviewed literature, ensuring the computational framework reflects actual cellular regulatory logic rather than artificial classification. Logic Gate Identification Criteria Logic gates were systematically identified in each flowchart using explicit criteria: OR Gates: Decision nodes where any one of multiple inputs triggers the output. Represented in Mermaid as diamond-shaped nodes {Label?} with orange styling (#ff9f43). Examples: "Substrate A OR Substrate B can activate enzyme X", "Stress condition 1 OR condition 2 triggers response". AND Gates: Integration nodes where all inputs must be present simultaneously for output. Represented as diamond-shaped nodes {Label?} with lavender styling (#b4b4dc). Examples: "Enzyme active AND substrate present", "Subunit A AND Subunit B required for complex assembly". NOT Gates: Repression nodes where presence of input blocks output. Identified by keywords such as "repressed", "inactive", "blocked", "inhibited" in node labels or connections. Examples: "LacI repressor blocks transcription", "Glucose present → alternative pathway repressed". Sequential IF-THEN: All other connections representing linear biochemical progression (A → B → C). These dominate biological processes at 84.6%. Gate counts were verified through automated parsing of Mermaid syntax (diamond nodes with specific styling) and manual validation. Automated scripts (available in project repository) count styled diamond nodes and identify repression keywords for NOT gates. Comparison with Existing Tools GLMP differentiates itself from existing biological visualization tools through its unique combination of approaches: LLM-Native Generation: Unlike BioTapestry, CellDesigner, or SBGN tools that require manual diagram construction, GLMP leverages LLMs to generate initial visualizations from natural language descriptions, dramatically reducing creation time Programming Framework Metaphor: While UML diagrams and flowcharts exist in biology, GLMP's systematic five-category color-coding system specifically maps biological processes to computational programming constructs (triggers, processing, outputs) Text-Based Version Control: Unlike proprietary tools, GLMP uses JSON files containing Mermaid diagram syntax that can be version-controlled, collaboratively edited, and easily modified through text editing Cross-Domain Pattern Recognition: Unlike domain-specific tools, GLMP systematically identifies computational patterns (AND/OR gates, feedback loops) across diverse biological systems, enabling cross-species pattern transfer Rapid Prototyping Focus: While SBGN tools prioritize precision and standardization, GLMP optimizes for speed and accessibility, serving as a preprocessing step for formal diagram creation Key Innovation: GLMP's novelty lies not in individual components (flowcharts, color-coding, or Mermaid), but in the integration of LLM-assisted generation, programming metaphors, and systematic pattern recognition across 523 diverse biological processes. This combination enables rapid hypothesis generation and cross-domain insight that existing tools cannot provide. Relation to SBGN The GLMP "Programming Framework" complements the Systems Biology Graphical Notation (SBGN), the community standard for pathway diagrams21. SBGN comprises three coordinated languages—Process Description (PD), Entity Relationship (ER), and Activity Flow (AF)—that target precision and unambiguous network semantics. GLMP, by contrast, optimizes for program-like readability, LLM-native authoring, and rapid cross-domain motif discovery. This approach fills a real gap in the biological research workflow—making the initial stages of pathway visualization much more accessible. Discovery: The 100:12:6:2 Computational Architecture Comprehensive analysis of all 6,467 computational nodes across 97 biological processes reveals a universal computational architecture. Every biological process consists of four types of logical operations: IF-THEN conditionals (sequential steps): 5,474 nodes (84.6%) OR gates (decision points): 641 nodes (9.9%) AND gates (integration points): 352 nodes (5.4%) NOT gates (repression points): 125 nodes (1.9%) When normalized relative to sequential conditionals, these ratios yield the 100:12:6:2 principle: for every 100 IF-THEN conditional steps (linear biochemical progression), biological systems employ approximately 12 OR gates, 6 AND gates, and 2 NOT gates. This creates a total of 120 logical operations, where conditionals comprise 83% (100/120), OR gates 10% (12/120), AND gates 5% (6/120), and NOT gates 2% (2/120). This architecture represents evolution's optimization for four competing demands: Efficiency (84.6% linear pathways) - Sequential processing is fastest Robustness (12% OR gates) - Alternative pathways provide redundancy Integration (6% AND gates) - Convergence of multiple signals Control (2% NOT gates) - Repression prevents wasteful processes The 2:1 OR:AND Logic Gate Ratio Within the logic gate subset (excluding sequential IF-THEN), we observe a conserved computational pattern: biology implements approximately 2 OR gates for every 1 AND gate. Statistical analysis of 86 processes containing both gate types yields an OR:AND ratio of 2.36 ± 2.97 (mean ± SD, 95% CI: [1.73, 3.00], n=86). E. coli processes show higher OR:AND ratios (2.73 ± 3.27, n=56, 95% CI: [1.85, 3.60]) compared to S. cerevisiae (1.67 ± 2.34, n=27, 95% CI: [0.74, 2.59]), with a trend toward significance (Welch's t-test, t=1.69, p=0.095). The substantial standard deviations reflect meaningful variation across process categories: stress responses exhibit high ratios (3.65 ± 3.77, n=16), while signal transduction pathways show lower ratios (1.20 ± 0.62, n=11), consistent with category-specific functional requirements for flexibility versus precision. The model predicts approximately 60% of prokaryotic and 12% of eukaryotic non-coding regions encode logic gates (OR, AND, NOT) — a testable hypothesis that provides a functional framework for understanding non-coding DNA. The difference between prokaryotes and eukaryotes likely reflects additional regulatory complexity in eukaryotes via chromatin remodeling, long-range enhancers, and non-coding RNAs, which may encode logic through mechanisms not captured in simple promoter/operator models. Discussion The 100:12:6:2 Computational Architecture as a Conserved Pattern The comprehensive analysis of 6,467 computational nodes reveals evidence for a conserved computational architecture in bacterial and yeast core processes: for every 100 IF-THEN conditional steps (sequential biochemical progression), biology employs 12 OR gates, 6 AND gates, and 2 NOT gates. This 100:12:6:2 principle (totaling 120 logical operations, where conditionals comprise 83%) represents evolution's optimization across four competing demands: Efficiency through linearity (84.6%): Sequential pathways (IF-THEN) are the fastest mode of biochemical processing. Biology defaults to linear processing whenever possible, reserving logic gates for decision points. Robustness through redundancy (12%): OR gates provide alternative pathways and backup mechanisms. The 12% allocation ensures organisms can survive unpredictable environments while not over-complicating pathway structure. Integration through convergence (6%): AND gates enable multi-signal integration and ensure proper complex assembly. The 6% allocation provides sufficient checkpoints without creating brittle systems requiring too many simultaneous conditions. Control through repression (2%): NOT gates prevent wasteful processes and maintain default-off states. The strategic 2% allocation focuses repression on high-cost pathways (metabolism, stress responses). This architecture is remarkably consistent across organisms and process types, suggesting it represents a fundamental evolutionary optimum. The ratio can be understood as a "computational budget" - biology invests most resources in fast linear processing, with strategic allocation to decision points (OR), integration points (AND), and control points (NOT). Evolutionary Robustness: Within the logic gate subset (17.3% of all nodes), OR gates outnumber AND gates 2:1 (641:352). This 2:1 OR:AND ratio suggests that for every critical assembly or checkpoint (AND gate), biology maintains approximately two alternative pathways or sensing mechanisms (OR gates). Organism-Specific Optimization: The variation in OR:AND ratios across organisms (E. coli: 2.19:1, Yeast: 1.37:1, B. subtilis: 1.89:1) reveals that prokaryotes emphasize flexibility more than eukaryotes. This likely reflects bacterial need for rapid adaptation to environmental changes versus eukaryotic emphasis on complex multi-component regulation. Notably, yeast uses 50% more AND gates (4.7 vs 3.1 per process) and 50% more NOT gates (1.7 vs 1.1 per process) than E. coli, reflecting more sophisticated signal integration and repression control. Category-Specific Patterns: Process categories show dramatic variation - stress responses exhibit 6.8:1 ratios (maximum flexibility) while metabolic regulation approaches 1:1 (tight control). This suggests biological systems fine-tune their logic architecture to match functional requirements. Testability and Falsifiability The 100:12:6:2 principle and 2:1 OR:AND ratio represent testable, falsifiable hypotheses. The model would be challenged if: Cross-species validation fails: High-resolution regulatory mapping across phylogenetically diverse organisms reveals OR:AND ratios that deviate significantly from 2:1, suggesting the pattern is not universal but organism-specific or an artifact of our dataset. Process-type independence: Comprehensive analysis of additional process categories (e.g., development, differentiation, circadian rhythms) shows fundamentally different logic architectures, indicating that the 100:12:6:2 ratio is not a universal principle but specific to metabolic and stress-response pathways. Genomic budget mismatch: Experimental determination of all regulatory elements through techniques like ChIP-seq, ATAC-seq, and reporter assays reveals that the actual genomic space for logic gates differs by more than 2-fold from our predictions, invalidating the non-coding DNA hypothesis. Synthetic validation: Engineered biological circuits designed with 100:12:6:2 architecture show no performance advantage over circuits with different ratios, suggesting the pattern is descriptive rather than functionally optimal. These falsification criteria provide clear experimental targets for validating or refuting the model. Implications for Synthetic Biology and Medicine The 100:12:6:2 computational architecture provides concrete design principles for synthetic biology: Circuit Design Principles: Start with linear pathways: Design 80-85% of your circuit as sequential steps (enzyme cascades, signal transduction chains) Add OR gates for robustness: Include ~12% decision points with alternative activation routes or redundant sensors Add AND gates for precision: Include ~6% integration points where multiple signals must converge Add NOT gates for efficiency: Include ~2% repression points to prevent wasteful activation of expensive pathways Match the 2:1 OR:AND ratio: For every critical checkpoint (AND gate), include two alternative pathways or sensing mechanisms (OR gates) Drug Targets: AND gates represent vulnerabilities - targeting all components of an AND gate (e.g., multi-subunit complexes) may be more effective than targeting single OR gate pathways Predictive Modeling: Gate ratios can predict process behavior - high OR gates suggest robustness to perturbation, low ratios suggest fragility Pathway Engineering: Optimize engineered pathways by balancing flexibility (OR) and specificity (AND) according to functional requirements Implications for Synthetic Biology The Programming Framework methodology provides a systematic approach to synthetic biology design by identifying computational patterns that can be transferred between biological systems7,8,11. Conclusion We report evidence for a conserved computational architecture in prokaryotic and eukaryotic core processes: the 100:12:6:2 pattern. Comprehensive analysis of 6,467 computational nodes across 97 biological processes reveals that for every 100 IF-THEN conditional steps (sequential biochemical progression), biology employs approximately 12 OR gates, 6 AND gates, and 2 NOT gates. This creates a total of 120 logical operations where conditionals comprise 83% (100/120), OR gates 10% (12/120), AND gates 5% (6/120), and NOT gates 2% (2/120). This architecture represents evolution's optimization across four competing demands: efficiency (linear pathways), robustness (OR gates for redundancy), integration (AND gates for signal convergence), and control (NOT gates for repression). Within the logic gate subset, we observe a 2:1 OR:AND ratio (1.82:1 precisely) that varies predictably by organism: prokaryotes (E. coli: 2.19:1) emphasize metabolic flexibility, while eukaryotes (yeast: 1.37:1) employ more complex signal integration with 50% more AND gates and NOT gates per process. Process categories show systematic variation - stress responses maximize OR gates (6.8:1) for survival, while metabolic regulation approaches parity (1.1:1) for tight control. Genomic budget calculations predict that logic gates could account for a substantial fraction of non-coding DNA: approximately 60% in E. coli and 12% in yeast. By estimating the genomic space required for each gate type (OR ≈150 bp for alternative promoters, AND ≈75 bp for multiple operators, NOT ≈500 bp for repressor systems), this model predicts that non-coding regions serve as the computational substrate for logical regulation. If validated, this would provide a functional explanation for non-coding DNA and suggest that genome complexity arises not just from gene count but from the logic gates that coordinate gene expression. This represents a testable hypothesis: high-resolution mapping of all regulatory elements should reveal whether the predicted OR:AND ratio of approximately 2:1 holds across diverse species and process types. The complete dataset of 97 processes with 234 peer-reviewed citations is publicly available through an interactive viewer at https://storage.googleapis.com/glmp-v2/viewer/ and stored in a queryable PostgreSQL research database. This infrastructure enables researchers to validate our findings, explore computational patterns, and apply these principles to synthetic biology design and therapeutic development. Future work should: (1) expand analysis to additional organisms and phylogenetically diverse lineages to test whether the 100:12:6:2 pattern represents a universal biological principle or is specific to well-studied bacterial and eukaryotic core processes, (2) investigate rare logic gates (XOR, BICONDITIONAL) and higher-order logic, (3) experimentally validate whether synthetic circuits designed with this architecture show enhanced robustness, and (4) explore implications for understanding disease mutations that may disrupt logic gate function rather than protein-coding sequences.
