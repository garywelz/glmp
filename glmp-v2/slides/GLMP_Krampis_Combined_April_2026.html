Big idea: you can author diagrams — tools like Mermaid (and LLM assistants) help you think before you pipette or queue a sequencing run.

The message for this class

  • A well-designed perturbation experiment starts from a clear picture of what could change when you intervene.
  • Mermaid Markdown (and similar “diagrams as code” tools) let you build, revise, and share flowcharts and logic quickly — including with generative-AI help.
  • The skill is the tool stack + critical reading, not reliance on any one person’s database or corpus.
  • Different prompts and models → different diagrams for the same biology — that’s normal; validate against textbooks and curated databases.

Alignment with transcriptomics & AI (this course)

You are learning to predict and interpret cellular responses to perturbations from bulk and single-cell data. A lightweight flowchart is not a virtual cell — it is a transparent hypothesis sketch: branches, gates, and “what should move first”.

  • Use it to choose what to perturb and what to measure (genes, programs, timepoints).
  • Use it to sanity-check model outputs: does the direction of change make sense for this mechanism?

Logic charts complement the transcriptomics + AI stack — feature matrices, deep models, virtual-cell lectures — they do not replace them.

What is Mermaid?

  • Text → diagram: you write Markdown-like code; a renderer draws flowcharts, sequences, state machines, and more.
  • Portable: GitHub/GitLab, Notion, many IDEs, Mermaid Live Editor, static sites — same source file travels with your project.
  • Collaborative: diffs are readable; easy to iterate after a lab meeting.
  • LLM-friendly: models can emit Mermaid blocks from a plain-language ask — then you edit and verify.

Example: lac operon (from a conversational prompt)

  • I asked a current LLM (e.g. Claude) whether it could produce a Mermaid flowchart of lac operon biochemistry — the result was surprisingly complete.
  • It surfaced the two-input logic: lactose / allolactose for derepression and glucose / cAMP–CAP as a second “volume knob.”
  • It separated default OFF (repressor on operator) from low vs high induction (weak vs strong polymerase recruitment).
  • It included a feedback arc: lactose depleted → allolactose drops → repressor rebinds → genes off again.

That quality is not guaranteed every time — it depends on model, prompt, and date. Treat any auto-generated chart as a first draft.

The difference

V2 adds exactly one node that V1 does not: LacI gene expression — placed between the cAMP–CAP / “no CAP binding” region and the “Operator free AND CAP bound?” decision. It is the explicit step: transcription of lacI (outside the operon), which produces the repressor protein.

Why it matters

  • V1 (LLM / literature) still captures regulatory logic (repressor as an actor) but can elide lacI as its own first-class molecular event.
  • V2 (RegulonDB-informed backbone) follows the database habit of treating LacI as a transcription factor with explicit regulatory wiring — so lacI expression appears as a node.
  • For perturbation design: knocking down or tuning lacI is a real strategy — it belongs in the picture as an explicit lever, not only as “repressor exists.”

Takeaway for class: What does adding a database give that the LLM alone may not? — Structured completeness at the level of molecular entities, not only functional narrative — easy to show side-by-side in the demo.

V1 — LLM from literature

Primary question: How does the logic work?

  • From a conversational prompt to a current LLM
  • Two-input Boolean logic: lactose present and glucose absent → strong transcription
  • AND/OR gates as diamond nodes
  • Feedback: lactose depletion → allolactose drops → repressor rebinds → genes off
  • High node count — comprehensive functional narrative
  • What it misses: no standard gene IDs, no database-level curation

Treat as a first draft. Validate against literature and databases.

V2 — Hybrid (LLM + RegulonDB backbone)

Primary question: Logic + who exactly regulates what?

  • Adds “LacI gene expression” — the node V1 elided
  • lacI expressed from its own gene outside the operon; RegulonDB encodes this as a first-class entity
  • Keeps Boolean gates and feedback from V1
  • Strong default for perturbation design — mechanistic logic + database precision
  • Perturbing lacI expression requires that explicit node

Hybrid: databases add structured completeness; the LLM adds logical interpretation.

V3 — RegulonDB only

Primary question: Who regulates whom, and how?

  • Built from RegulonDB (e.g. v13) — reference-grade E. coli transcriptional regulation
  • Centers regulatory logic: allolactose relieves LacI; CRP activates via cAMP
  • Feedback explicit: lactose depletes → less allolactose → repressor rebinds
  • LacI as hub; lacZ, lacY, lacA as outputs; glucose + galactose as products where modeled
  • What it misses: no Boolean AND/OR encoding of the two-input requirement

RegulonDB knows who controls what — not always the simultaneous conditions both controls must satisfy.

V4 — EcoCyc only

Primary question: What are the molecular parts and what do they do?

  • Signature: operon as one LACOPERON-style unit
  • Enzymes named: lacZ (β-galactosidase), lacY (permease), lacA (transacetylase)
  • Edges: “binds LacI,” “represses,” “activates”
  • No feedback loop — parts catalog, not a full circuit cartoon
  • No decision gates — interactions, not conditional logic
  • Best for: components, molecular roles

EcoCyc tells you what the parts are — not how they combine under conditional logic.

V5 — KEGG only

Primary question: Which genes, which pathway, which identifiers?

  • Gene nodes with locus tags — e.g. eco:b1677 lacI, eco:b3356 crp, eco:b1675 / b1676 / b1674 for lacZ/Y/A
  • Outputs as Galactose and Glucose — pathway resolution
  • Regulation as simple edge labels (“repress,” “activate”)
  • No feedback, no Boolean gates
  • Best for: cross-organism alignment, IDs for pipelines and tools

KEGG is a gateway of stable identifiers — not a full mechanism cartoon.

V6 — Abasy only

Primary question: Which regulons control which genes?

  • Regulon-level abstraction — LacI regulon, CRP regulon, not single-protein detail
  • Fewest nodes, highest-level view
  • lacZ, lacY, lacA as outputs of the lac promoter region
  • No metabolic products, no feedback, no gates in this rendition
  • Best for: network / architecture comparisons across organisms

Abasy asks which master regulators control which gene sets — not every molecular edge.

Critical insight

Only V1 and V2 encode the AND gate

The two-input condition:

Lactose must be present and glucose must be absent for strong transcription.

  • That conditional dependence is not “stored” as an AND gate in the databases — they record facts; combining them under specific conditions is interpretation from explanatory literature.
  • The LLM supplies that interpretive layer in V1/V2.
  • Perturbation: effect of a crp perturbation can depend on glucose — the AND structure makes that visible; V3–V6 as shown do not.

Workflow

One-sentence distillation + workflow

The databases tell you the parts; the LLM tells you the logic; the hybrid gives you both — and the logic is what determines which perturbations are informative.

Suggested workflow:

  1. Start with V1 — grasp mechanism and logic
  2. Check V3 or V4 — validate regulation and parts
  3. Use V5 — lock in standard identifiers for tools and models
  4. Build toward V2 — merge into a validated hybrid when you need both
  5. Design perturbations — AND inputs, branch points, feedback nodes

Method

Diagram variation is scientific information

Six versions of one operon show:

  • What each resource was designed to represent
  • What each cannot represent by design
  • Where sources agree — high-confidence anchors
  • Where they diverge — level of description or open questions

Reconciling diagrams from different sources against a third reference is the same core skill as comparing RNA-seq pipelines or virtual-cell model outputs.

Mermaid + databases + LLMs + your judgment = a reproducible, inspectable hypothesis before you spend on the experiment.

Hybrid strategy

Databases and LLMs: orthogonal contributions

  • V2 hybrid added one node — LacI gene expression — that is a real perturbation target; V1 alone could elide it.
  • V3–V6 (database-first views) as shown do not encode the full AND gate (lactose present and glucose absent) — that conditional logic comes from interpretation of mechanism, not from storing disconnected facts.
  • Databases excel at entities and wiring; LLMs excel at explicit logical structure (gates, feedback stories) when guided and validated.

The databases tell you the parts; the LLM tells you the logic; the hybrid gives you both — what you need for many perturbation designs.

Hybrid strategy

Why not merge all databases into one chart?

Each was built for a different abstraction level. Naively unioning them mixes ontologies — you can lose the very clarity that makes V1/V2 useful.

Hybrid strategy

Layered hybridization (not one giant merge)

  1. Structural partner: a regulatory database at TF→gene resolution (for E. coli, RegulonDB-class). Same abstraction level as many LLM charts — adds entity completeness without shredding gates. This is the V2 lesson.
  2. Parts audit: EcoCyc / BioCyc — check names, reactions, products; do not necessarily merge every edge structurally.
  3. Identifier layer: KEGG — attach locus tags as metadata on nodes after topology is stable; good for pipelines and cross-species alignment.
  4. Architecture check: Abasy — confirm regulon-level story is consistent with your chart; treat as a zoomed-out view, not every node in one picture.

Research directions

What many-chart comparisons could quantify

  • Entity recovery: recurring class of nodes LLMs skip (TF expression steps, autoregulation, cofactors) that regulatory DBs restore — lacI may be a pattern, not a one-off.
  • Logic-preservation test: do V1 AND/OR gates and feedback survive careful hybridization? Systematic yes/no across charts stress-tests prompts.
  • Agreement vs divergence: shared topology → higher confidence; divergence → LLM slip, literature gap, or DB boundary — worth cataloguing.
  • Organism gradient: deep regulation DBs (e.g. E. coli) reward hybridization; sparse systems lean on LLM + literature with higher stated uncertainty.

Risks

When LLM “garbling” is most likely

  • Prompts that force simultaneous reconciliation of contradictory or incompatible database views
  • Dumping large raw dumps instead of curated relationships the model can map
  • Pressure to be maximally comprehensive — every edge at once — so conditional logic washes out

Mitigation (V2-style): database as backbone for entities and regulatory wiring; LLM role = interpret and draw logic — keep roles explicit in the prompt.

Take-home

Bottom line for hybrid workflows

  • The V1–V6 comparison is a concrete demo: source choice changes the chart in classifiable ways; hybrid can target perturbation design better than any single source alone.
  • Productive default: LLM logic + one regulatory database per organism as the main structural partner; other resources as validation, identifiers, and architecture checks — not a single overloaded merger.
  • Aim for consistent level of description in the picture you will actually use to plan experiments.

Prompts change the picture

  • Same organism, same pathway — different wording (detail level, audience, “include CAP or not,” stress on metabolism vs regulation) → different nodes and layout.
  • No single “official” Mermaid chart exists for a biological story; there are better and worse matches to evidence.
  • Good practice: generate two versions with two prompts, diff them, reconcile against a trusted source.
  • Your lab notebook can store both the Mermaid source and the citation (review article, RegulonDB page, etc.).

Use the tools

  • Build a habit of using the right tools — literature, databases, diagram-as-code — to reveal the explicit structure of what you are researching (inputs, branch points, feedback) before you sink cost into experiments or large models.
  • GLMP (and similar projects) are optional galleries of how others styled logic-heavy biology in Mermaid.
  • What scales is: Mermaid (or equivalent) + literature + databases + your judgment.

What people lean on to design perturbations

Most projects mix literature + structured databases + organism-specific tools. Here is a practical bucket list (usable in any system you study).

1. Primary literature & reviews

  • Target choice: which gene, complex, or pathway to disturb — expected phenotype or expression signature
  • Mechanism: feedback, redundancy, condition dependence (e.g. only under low oxygen)
  • Where: PubMed / Europe PMC; Nature Reviews; specialist journals; Perturb-seq–style methods papers for design patterns

Resource buckets (continued)

2. Pathway & interaction context (“what touches what”)

  • Reactome, KEGG, WikiPathways, BioCyc / EcoCyc (strong for E. coli metabolism)
  • STRING — protein–protein / functional links
  • Answers: If I hit node A, what is one step away? What module am I in?

3. Gene-centric facts (identifiers, knockouts, orthology)

  • NCBI Gene, Ensembl, UniProt, Gene Ontology
  • Model-organism DBs: e.g. SGD (yeast); fly/worm bases when relevant
  • Answers: Essential? Paralog? Standard name?

Resource buckets (continued)

4. Regulatory & context-specific layers

  • Often underused early on: RegulonDB-class resources — e.g. RegulonDB for E. coli transcriptional regulation; TF–target summaries in reviews; atlases such as Abasy
  • Answers: Why might the same KO differ by medium, oxygen, or growth phase?

5. Perturbation modalities & reagent design (the “how”)

  • CRISPR (KO, CRISPRi, CRISPRa), RNAi, ORF libraries, small molecules where chemistry exists
  • Pointers: Addgene, virus/application notes, genome-wide library papers — not always one tidy “database”

Resource buckets (continued)

6. Data-first inspiration (screens & atlases)

  • Expression archives, screen compendia, GEO, single-cell portals, cohort atlases
  • Answers: What readout is realistic? Saturated vs focused design?

7. Organisms

  • Human / cell lines: common for Perturb-seq–style work and disease framing
  • E. coli, yeast: clean genetics, mapping, teaching causal chains — regulatory motifs often generalize conceptually even when every organism in the syllabus isn’t covered by one corpus

How logic flowcharts supplement those resources

Databases and papers excel at coverage and citation; they often do not give one integrated, inspectable story of this process under these regulatory conditions for teaching and debate.

What typical resources give What logic-style flowcharts add
Lists of genes/reactions & standard pathway maps Explicit logic & narrative flow — branching, AND/OR-style reasoning, “under what condition does this path matter?”
Static maps optimized for lookup A teaching-oriented layout for discourse — “if we cut this edge, what breaks first?”
Interaction confidence from omics Hypothesis structure — which steps are modeled as coupled (assumptions stated clearly)
Methods for executing perturbations A perturbation menu: inputs, master regulators, branch points, feedback — suggest informative rather than arbitrary interventions

One-sentence distillation

“Databases tell you what is connected; papers tell you what was measured; a logic flowchart helps you choose perturbations that disambiguate mechanisms and predict qualitative directions before you spend money and time on the experiment or on fitting a large model.”

That frames interpretive structure as sitting on top of the usual evidence stack — complementary to transcriptomics + AI + virtual-cell themes, not competing with them.

From flowchart to experiment

  • Mark inputs you can actually manipulate (nutrients, inducers, knockouts, CRISPRi).
  • Mark branch points — perturb there to see which downstream arm the cell uses.
  • Turn ovals/boxes into measurement targets: RNA of which program? reporter? growth?
  • Expect to revise the chart after data — that loop is the science.

Takeaways

  • Know the seven resource buckets — mix literature, DBs, reagents, and data compendia for your organism.
  • Lac V1–V6: AND gate mainly in V1/V2; layered hybridization beats merging every DB into one graph.
  • Learn Mermaid (or similar) — practical for experiment design in the AI era.
  • LLMs draft diagrams — you validate; prompt variation = diagram variation.
  • Tools > any single corpus. Logic charts complement databases and papers; they do not replace them.
