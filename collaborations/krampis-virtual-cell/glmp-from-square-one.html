DNA contains not just the recipes for proteins, but the logic that controls when and where those recipes are used — and we can read that logic computationally, the same way a compiler reads a program.

The problem GLMP solves

Biologists have spent decades identifying individual transcription factors and their binding sites. But the field lacks a systematic way to represent the logical relationships between them — the AND gates, OR gates, feedback loops, and conditional switches that make a cell respond intelligently to its environment.

GLMP proposes that regulatory DNA has a grammar: transcription factor binding motifs are the alphabet, their spatial arrangement is the syntax, and the logical operations (AND / OR / NOT / IF-THEN) are the semantics. If that's right, we can read any regulatory sequence as a logical formula.

This is the inverse of what Voigt et al. (2016, Science) demonstrated for synthetic biology — they showed that logic programs can be compiled into novel DNA sequences that execute gate-level circuits inside living E. coli. GLMP reads the logic that evolution has already written into natural regulatory DNA. Same grammar, opposite direction.

The five-class complexity ladder

GLMP organizes regulatory circuits into five classes of increasing complexity:

ClassStructureBiological example
IFeed-forward, no feedbackSimple inducible promoter
IISingle negative feedback looptrp operon (TrpR end-product repression)
IIIBistable switch / positive feedbackara operon (AraC autoactivation); lac operon (disputed — see below)
IVDelayed negative feedback — oscillatoryCircadian clock (CLOCK/BMAL1)
VSelf-modifying — circuit alters its own architectureDNMT3A self-methylation

This ladder is the backbone of the GLMP corpus. Every decoded circuit gets classified here. Class III circuits are empirically harder for AI perturbation models to predict — a finding with direct implications for the RegVelo and K562 work.

The DNA Decoder — what we built and what it does

The Decoder is a software pipeline running on a Jetson Nano (edge compute device) that takes a DNA promoter sequence and produces a typed logical flowchart:

DNA sequence (FASTA)
        ↓
FIMO motif scanner
(identifies transcription factor binding sites using JASPAR 2024 — 2,346 motifs)
        ↓
GLMP Logic Parser
(interprets spatial arrangement of sites as AND / OR / NOT logic gates)
        ↓
Mermaid flowchart + circuit class assignment (I–V) + Firestore write

The one open question we need help with

Before the decoded circuits can be used as ground truth for training or validation, we need expert biological review to confirm that the gate assignments are correct.

We have prepared an annotation review document with:

  • Rendered flowcharts for all three decoded operons (lac, ara, trp)
  • Explicit binding site sequences and genomic coordinates
  • Specific reviewer questions for each entry
  • Primary literature citations for every factual claim

What we are asking from the Krampis lab

Two distinct workstreams — one biological, one computational:

Workstream A — Biological validation (biology-strong student)

  • Phase 1 (weeks 1–3): Cross-reference FIMO-decoded binding sites against RegulonDB gold-standard database for E. coli — do our predicted sites match known experimentally validated sites?
  • Output: overlap statistics for lac, ara, trp; confirmation or correction of circuit class assignments

Workstream B — Computational / single-cell (computation-strong student)

  • RegVelo on K562 — inferring gene regulatory dynamics from single-cell RNA-seq data
  • RPE1 replication timing work
  • Stretch goal: Evo 2 cross-validation via Arc Institute API (weeks 4–8)
  • Output: empirically grounded circuits from human cell data, extending GLMP beyond E. coli

Key question for the meeting: Does your student have both biological and computational background, or primarily one? If one person can cover both workstreams, great. If not, the fall students may be the right people for the second stream.

The bigger picture

The long-term goal is a unified knowledge engine where a researcher queries a biological process — say, "lac operon" — and gets back: a logic-gate flowchart of the circuit, the source papers that ground it, the DNA decoding of the regulatory sequence, and eventually a podcast and knowledge graph. GLMP is the first working component of that engine. The CopernicusAI platform (60,780 research papers, growing daily) is the second. The validation work we are asking for now is what connects the two — it's the quality control layer that makes the decoded circuits trustworthy enough to publish and build on.
